Where Basement Water Comes From

Water enters in a few predictable ways.

Wall cracks

Hydrostatic pressure pushes groundwater through cracks and joints.

Floor-wall joint

The seam where the slab meets the wall is a common seep point.

Window wells

Poor drainage lets water spill over and in.

Plumbing leaks

A leaking pipe can look like a wall seep. See leak detection.

Waterproofing Options Compared

The right fix depends on the cause.

Gutters, Grading and Window Wells

Many damp basements start outside. Downspouts that dump water at the foundation, soil that slopes toward the wall and window wells without drains all push water toward the house.

Extend downspouts several feet from the foundation, keep the soil sloping away and clear window wells. These small changes cost little and often cut the amount of water the basement has to handle.

A River Edge and a Water Table

The Little Calumet River forms Lansing's northern edge, and northeast Lansing flooded in 2008 after a levee failure near the Munster border. A levee restoration plan protects an estimated 2,500 residential and 20 business structures across the project area.

Basements near the river or in low spots have a higher chance of seeping during wet seasons. A sump pump with a backup is a basic part of the plan. See sump pump installation and repair.
