How Tankless Differs From a Tank

A tankless unit fires only when a hot tap opens. It gives a steady stream and frees up floor space, but it has a maximum flow, so running several showers at once can exceed it.

The trade-off is the supply work. Tankless units often need a larger gas line or a heavy electric circuit, and special venting, so the install is more involved than swapping a tank.

Tankless or Tank: A Practical Comparison

Neither option is better for every house. This is how the two usually stack up.

Tank heater

  • Lower purchase and install cost
  • Simple venting and supply needs
  • Stored water can run out in a long use

Tankless heater

  • Hot water on demand with no storage
  • Saves floor space
  • Needs correct sizing, venting and routine descaling

Maintenance That Keeps a Tankless Unit Working

Scale is the main enemy of a tankless heater. A short routine helps.

1

Test your tap

Ask your water utility for its annual water quality report, or use a home test kit, to learn how mineral-heavy your water is.

2

Flush on a schedule

Descaling flushes the heat exchanger with a cleaning solution. The manufacturer's manual gives the interval.

3

Clean the inlet screen

A clogged screen cuts flow and can trigger error codes.

4

Check venting

Make sure vent pipes are clear and sealed, especially after a storm.

Cold Winters and Tankless Performance

In winter the water entering a house is much colder than in summer, so a tankless unit has to add more heat to reach the same temperature. That can lower the flow it can deliver at a set temperature, which is why sizing for the coldest months matters.

Ask the plumber to size the unit for winter use rather than summer, and to check that the gas supply can carry the load on a cold day. A unit sized only for mild weather can disappoint in January.
